## What is Social Security and How Does It Work?

Social Security is a federal program that provides retirement, disability, and survivor benefits to eligible Americans. It is primarily funded through payroll taxes under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA). Understanding how these benefits are calculated and distributed can significantly impact your financial planning.

### How Are Social Security Benefits Calculated?

Benefits are based on your average indexed monthly earnings (AIME) during your 35 highest-earning years. The Social Security Administration (SSA) uses this to determine your primary insurance amount (PIA), which is the basis for your benefit calculations. For those nearing retirement, it’s crucial to verify your earnings record with the SSA to ensure accuracy.

## When Can You Start Receiving Social Security Benefits?

### Age and Timing Considerations

You can start receiving Social Security retirement benefits as early as age 62, but doing so will reduce your monthly benefit. Full retirement age (FRA) varies depending on your birth year, ranging between 66 and 67. Delaying benefits beyond your FRA can increase your monthly benefit by up to 8% per year until age 70—a strategy known as "delayed retirement credits."

### Should You Delay Benefits?

Deciding when to start benefits depends on several factors, including your health, financial needs, and life expectancy. For instance, if you expect to live longer than average, delaying benefits might maximize your lifetime income. Conversely, if you need immediate income or have health concerns, starting earlier could be beneficial.

## What Are Spousal and Survivor Benefits?

### Understanding Spousal Benefits

Spouses of eligible workers can receive up to 50% of the worker’s PIA. This can be particularly advantageous for those who have lower lifetime earnings. However, claiming spousal benefits before your FRA will reduce the amount you receive.

### Survivor Benefits Explained

Widows and widowers can claim survivor benefits as early as age 60, or 50 if they are disabled. These benefits can be up to 100% of the deceased spouse’s PIA. It’s important to coordinate survivor benefits with your own retirement benefits to optimize the total amount received.

## How Does Working Affect Your Social Security?

### The Earnings Test

If you start receiving benefits before your FRA and continue to work, your benefits may be temporarily reduced depending on your earnings. For 2026, benefits will be reduced by $1 for every $2 earned over a set threshold until the year you reach your FRA. After that, benefits are recalculated to account for any months in which they were reduced.

### Strategic Considerations

For those planning to work while collecting benefits, it’s essential to understand how the earnings test can impact your total income. Consider the potential tax implications and how continued work might increase your future benefits through additional contributions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the full retirement age for Social Security?

The full retirement age varies by birth year. For those born between 1943 and 1954, it is 66. It gradually increases to 67 for those born in 1960 or later.

### Can I receive Social Security benefits if I continue to work?

Yes, but if you’re under your full retirement age, earnings above a certain threshold may temporarily reduce your benefits. After reaching your FRA, there is no reduction regardless of your earnings.

### How does divorce affect Social Security benefits?

If you were married for at least 10 years and are now divorced, you may be eligible for spousal benefits based on your ex-spouse’s record, provided you are unmarried and meet other criteria.

### Will my Social Security benefits be taxed?

Yes, Social Security benefits may be taxable depending on your total income. Up to 85% of your benefits can be subject to federal taxation if your income exceeds certain thresholds.

### Can I change my Social Security claiming decision after starting benefits?

You can withdraw your application within 12 months of first claiming, but you must repay any benefits received. Alternatively, you can suspend benefits once you reach FRA to earn delayed retirement credits.
